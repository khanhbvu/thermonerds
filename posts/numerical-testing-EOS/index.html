Numerical Testing of an EOS Implementation

thermodynamics
equations-of-state
srk
numerics
testing

A perturbation test that catches a wrong derivative whether you derived it by hand or let a computer do it, worked through on SRK.

Published

October 31, 2026

Working with an equation of state means differentiating it, repeatedly. You need pressure as a derivative of Helmholtz energy, chemical potentials as derivatives with respect to composition, fugacity coefficients built from those, and second derivatives on top for anything that solves by Newton’s method. The expressions are considerably worse than polynomials, and in most production code they are derived by hand and then coded — autodiff costs a factor of several per evaluation, and a flash calculation calls these functions millions of times.

Both steps can go wrong, and the failure is quiet. A derivative that is wrong by half a percent produces numbers that look entirely plausible. Your flash calculation still converges — just more slowly, or to the wrong answer near a phase boundary, or not at all at the one state point you needed.

What makes this tractable is that you do not need a second implementation to check against, and you do not need symbolic algebra. You can check a derivative against the only thing you are sure of: the function it claims to differentiate.

Where \(E_1\) and \(E_2\) come from

Take any function \(F(x)\) and perturb its argument to \(x_{pert} = x + \Delta x\). A Taylor expansion says exactly what happens:

\[ F(x + \Delta x) = F(x) + \frac{dF}{dx}\Delta x + \frac{1}{2}\frac{d^2F}{dx^2}(\Delta x)^2 + \frac{1}{6}\frac{d^3F}{dx^3}(\Delta x)^3 + \cdots \tag{1}\]

Now truncate Equation 1 at successive orders and ask how wrong you are each time. That is the whole idea, and both \(E_1\) and \(E_2\) come straight out of it.

Truncate after the first term. Pretend \(F\) does not change at all, so \(F(x_{pert}) \approx F(x)\). The error you make is everything you discarded:

\[ E_1 \equiv F(x_{pert}) - F(x) = \frac{dF}{dx}\Delta x + \frac{1}{2}\frac{d^2F}{dx^2}(\Delta x)^2 + \cdots \tag{2}\]

Truncate after the second term. Now use the derivative you are testing. The error left over is:

\[ E_2 \equiv F(x_{pert}) - F(x) - \frac{dF}{dx}\Delta x = E_1 - \frac{dF}{dx}\Delta x = \frac{1}{2}\frac{d^2F}{dx^2}(\Delta x)^2 + \cdots \tag{3}\]

So \(E_1\) and \(E_2\) are the same kind of object — truncation errors of the same series — measured one order apart. That is the entire construction.

Two observations before going further.

Neither is zero, and neither is supposed to be. Chasing a small \(E_2\) is the wrong instinct; what carries the information is the rate at which each one vanishes as \(\Delta x\) shrinks.

And note the asymmetry. \(E_1\) has a leading term plus corrections, so for small \(\Delta x\) you can legitimately drop everything after \(\frac{dF}{dx}\Delta x\). \(E_2\) cannot be simplified the same way — its leading term is the first surviving term, and there is nothing larger to compare it against.

Only one of these quantities contains your derivative. \(E_1\) is computed from two calls to \(F\) and nothing else, so it cannot be wrong. \(E_2\) is the only place your claim enters, which is why it is the one that tests it.

Why the slope is 2

Work it through on \(F = x^2\), where \(dF/dx = 2x\) and everything is visible:

\[ E_1 = (x + \Delta x)^2 - x^2 = 2x\Delta x + (\Delta x)^2 \tag{4}\]

\[ E_2 = E_1 - 2x\Delta x = (\Delta x)^2 \tag{5}\]

For small \(\Delta x\) the quadratic term in Equation 4 is negligible beside the linear one, so \(E_1 \approx 2x\Delta x\). Take logarithms of both:

\[ \ln|E_1| = \ln(2x) + \ln(\Delta x) \tag{6}\]

\[ \ln|E_2| = 2\ln(\Delta x) \tag{7}\]

Here \(x\) is held fixed and only \(\Delta x\) varies, so \(\ln(2x)\) is a constant. Eliminate \(\ln(\Delta x)\) between Equation 6 and Equation 7:

\[ \ln|E_2| = 2\ln|E_1| - 2\ln(2x) \tag{8}\]

A straight line of slope 2. Not approximately, not asymptotically — this is what the algebra gives, for a correctly coded derivative.

Why it fails loudly when the derivative is wrong

Suppose your code returns \(\frac{dF}{dx} + \delta\) instead of the true value. Then Equation 3 becomes

\[ E_2 = -\delta\,\Delta x + \frac{1}{2}\frac{d^2F}{dx^2}(\Delta x)^2 + \cdots \]

The leading term is now first order in \(\Delta x\), exactly like \(E_1\). So \(\ln|E_2| = \ln|E_1| + \text{constant}\) and the slope collapses to 1.

This is why the test is sharp. However small \(\delta\) is, \(\Delta x\) eventually beats \((\Delta x)^2\), so the wrong first-order term always wins in the limit. A tiny error does not produce a slope of 1.99 — it produces a slope of 1. You are reading a rate, not a magnitude, and there is no tolerance to tune.

The general case

Nothing above needed \(F = x^2\). Dividing Equation 3 by the square of Equation 2, the \(\Delta x\) factors cancel:

\[ \frac{E_2}{E_1^{\,2}} \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{F''(x)}{2\,F'(x)^2} \qquad \text{as } \Delta x \to 0 \tag{9}\]

so for any twice-differentiable \(F\),

\[ \ln|E_2| = 2\ln|E_1| + \ln\left|\frac{F''(x)}{2F'(x)^2}\right| + O(\Delta x) \tag{10}\]

Slope 2 always; the intercept depends on the function and on where you evaluate it. For \(F = x^2\) that intercept is \(\ln\left|2 / (2 \cdot 4x^2)\right| = -2\ln(2x)\), which is Equation 8 again.

The split matters: the intercept is what you would need to know the function to predict, and the slope is not. That is why the test is built on the slope.

Checking it on functions you can differentiate by hand

Two functions first, including one wrong derivative for contrast. For \(F = \ln x\) the series does not terminate — \(E_2 = \ln(1 + \Delta x/x) - \Delta x/x = -\tfrac{1}{2}(\Delta x/x)^2 + \cdots\) — so the \(O(\Delta x)\) correction in Equation 10 is visible at large steps and fades as the step shrinks.

Two correct derivatives give 2. A five percent error gives 1. Nothing lands in between, and the gap widens as the step shrinks rather than narrowing.

Five percent is an arbitrary choice, so the obvious question is whether the slope also reports how wrong the derivative is. It does not.

Every column converges on 1, and the larger errors arrive sooner — at the coarsest step a 50% error already reads 0.9980 while a 1% error is still at 0.9275. So the slope answers whether the derivative is wrong, not by how much. That is the useful behaviour for a test: a one percent error is as detectable as a catastrophic one, and you do not need to guess the size of the bug in advance to choose a step size.

Does it hold generally?

Two cases prove little, and Equation 10 claims something about every twice-differentiable function — including the intercept. A wider sweep:

Growth rates from \(1/x\) to \(e^x\), a sign-changing second derivative, a product needing the chain rule. Every slope is 2 to four decimals and every intercept matches Equation 10 to five. That is the case for trusting the method on something you cannot differentiate by hand.

Measuring the slope properly

Each perturbation produces exactly one \(E_1\) and one \(E_2\), so a series of step sizes produces a series of points — one point per step. Writing

\[ F_1 = \ln|E_1|, \qquad F_2 = \ln|E_2| \tag{11}\]

a run over five values of \(\Delta x\) gives five pairs \((F_1, F_2)\), and the slope you are testing is the slope of the line through them.

Why two points is the minimum

A single perturbation gives a single point, and a single point has no slope — infinitely many lines pass through it.

It is tempting to think the ratio \(F_2/F_1\) would do. It would, if the line passed through the origin. It does not: Equation 10 puts the intercept at \(\ln\left|F''/2F'^{\,2}\right|\), which for the worked example below is about 4.01. Taking the ratio anyway, on that same data:

\(p\) \(F_1\) \(F_2\) \(F_2/F_1\)
1e-02 1.1717 −5.1997 −4.4377
1e-03 −1.1324 −9.8044 8.6580
1e-04 −3.4351 −14.4095 4.1947
1e-05 −5.7377 −19.0149 3.3140

Not one of them is near 2, and they do not even agree with each other. The intercept is responsible for the whole mess, and it is not optional — it carries real information, since Equation 10 predicts it in closed form.

Two points remove the problem, because the unknown intercept cancels.

Do not assume the slope — it is the thing being measured. Call it \(m\), leave the intercept as \(c\), and write the relation at two step sizes \(k\) and \(k+1\):

\[ F_{2,k} = m F_{1,k} + c, \qquad F_{2,k+1} = m F_{1,k+1} + c \tag{12}\]

Two equations, two unknowns. Subtracting eliminates \(c\) whatever its value and leaves \(m\) on its own:

\[ m = \frac{F_{2,k} - F_{2,k+1}}{F_{1,k} - F_{1,k+1}} = \frac{\ln\left|E_{2,k} / E_{2,k+1}\right|}{\ln\left|E_{1,k} / E_{1,k+1}\right|} \tag{13}\]

This matters for the logic of the test. Equation 13 measures \(m\) from the data without assuming anything about it. Theory then predicts what it should be: \(m = 2\) if the derivative is correct, \(m = 1\) if it is not. Comparing the two is the test. Had we written \(2\) in Equation 12 we would have assumed the answer and learned nothing.

Equation 13 is what the slope columns in every table here compute. Each value belongs to a pair of rows, not to a single row — which is why those columns are labelled by an interval and why the last row has no entry.

So two points is the minimum. They determine a unique line, so they fix \(c\) as well as \(m\) — back-substituting \(c = F_{2,k} - m F_{1,k}\) recovers it. Equation 13 simply avoids needing it. That is a convenience, not a limitation.

What two points cannot give you is any evidence that the relation is linear in the first place. A line through two points fits them exactly, with zero residual, so there is nothing left over to tell you whether the slope is stable. Five perturbations give you that, and stability across the range is what locates the round-off floor.

Fitting all of them at once

Reading the slope a pair at a time is fine for seeing the trend, but each value uses only two of your points and depends on which pair you picked. Least squares uses all of them:

\[ \text{slope} = \frac{\sum_r F_{1,r}\left(F_{2,r} - \overline{F_2}\right)} {\sum_r F_{1,r}\left(F_{1,r} - \overline{F_1}\right)} \tag{14}\]

The subscript \(r\) indexes the perturbations — one term per step size — and the overbars are means over that series.

Where Equation 14 comes from

It is ordinary linear least squares, and it is worth seeing that this form is not a rearrangement of the familiar one — it is what the normal equations give directly. Fitting \(F_2 = a + b F_1\) means choosing \(a\) and \(b\) to minimise

\[ S(a, b) = \sum_r \left(F_{2,r} - a - b F_{1,r}\right)^2 \tag{15}\]

Differentiating with respect to the intercept and setting it to zero,

\[ \frac{\partial S}{\partial a} = -2\sum_r \left(F_{2,r} - a - b F_{1,r}\right) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a = \overline{F_2} - b\,\overline{F_1} \tag{16}\]

so the fitted line passes through the centroid of the data. Differentiating with respect to the slope,

\[ \frac{\partial S}{\partial b} = -2\sum_r F_{1,r}\left(F_{2,r} - a - b F_{1,r}\right) = 0 \tag{17}\]

and substituting Equation 16 into Equation 17 gives

\[ \sum_r F_{1,r}\left(F_{2,r} - \overline{F_2}\right) = b \sum_r F_{1,r}\left(F_{1,r} - \overline{F_1}\right) \]

which is Equation 14 solved for \(b\). The more commonly quoted \(\sum(F_1 - \overline{F_1})(F_2 - \overline{F_2}) \big/ \sum(F_1 - \overline{F_1})^2\) is the same number, obtained by adding and subtracting terms that vanish because deviations from a mean sum to zero.

One detail worth noticing: the intercept \(a\) is free here, not pinned to zero. That is deliberate — Equation 10 predicts a non-zero intercept, and forcing the line through the origin would bias the slope.

The worked example

SRK for a methane–ethane binary, as a residual Helmholtz function. With

\[ B = \sum_i n_i b_i, \qquad D = \sum_i\sum_j n_i n_j a_{ij}, \qquad g = \ln\left(1 - \frac{B}{V}\right), \qquad f = \frac{1}{B}\ln\left(1 + \frac{B}{V}\right) \]

the residual Helmholtz energy and the composition derivative under test are

\[ A^{res} = -n_{tot} R T\, g - D f \tag{18}\]

\[ A_{n_i} = -RT g - n_{tot} R T g_B b_i - D_i f - D f_B b_i \tag{19}\]

where \(g_B = -1/(V-B)\), \(f_V = -1/[V(V+B)]\), \(f_B = -(f + V f_V)/B\) and \(D_i = 2\sum_j a_{ij} n_j\).

Equation 19 is the reason this post exists. Four terms, each arriving by a different path through the chain rule, and no way to tell by looking whether it is right.

R, T, V = 8.314462618, 300.0, 500.0      # J/mol/K, K, cm3
Tc  = [190.56, 305.32]                    # K
Pc  = [4.599e6, 4.872e6]                  # Pa
w   = [0.011, 0.099]
n   = [0.6, 0.4]                          # mol
k12 = 0.01

b  = [8.664e4 * (tc * R) / pc for tc, pc in zip(Tc, Pc)]
ac = [4.2747e5 * ((R * tc) ** 2) / pc for tc, pc in zip(Tc, Pc)]
m  = [0.48 + 1.574 * z - 0.175 * z * z for z in w]
al = [(1 + mi * (1 - math.sqrt(T / tc))) ** 2 for mi, tc in zip(m, Tc)]
a  = [aci * ali for aci, ali in zip(ac, al)]
aij = [[a[0], math.sqrt(a[0] * a[1]) * (1 - k12)],
       [math.sqrt(a[0] * a[1]) * (1 - k12), a[1]]]

def A_res(nv):
    B = sum(ni * bi for ni, bi in zip(nv, b))
    D = sum(nv[i] * nv[j] * aij[i][j] for i in range(2) for j in range(2))
    return -sum(nv) * R * T * math.log(1 - B / V) - D * math.log(1 + B / V) / B

B = sum(ni * bi for ni, bi in zip(n, b))
D = sum(n[i] * n[j] * aij[i][j] for i in range(2) for j in range(2))
g, f = math.log(1 - B / V), math.log(1 + B / V) / B
g_B  = -1 / (V - B)
f_V  = -1 / (V * (V + B))
f_B  = -(f + V * f_V) / B
D1   = 2 * (aij[0][0] * n[0] + aij[0][1] * n[1])

A_n1 = -R * T * g - sum(n) * R * T * g_B * b[0] - D1 * f - D * f_B * b[0]

print(f"A_res = {A_res(n):.6f} J")
print(f"A_n1  = {A_n1:.6f} J/mol")
A_res = -406.946593 J
A_n1  = -537.005260 J/mol

Perturbing a mole number

One practical change from the theory above. Rather than a fixed \(\Delta x\), perturb relatively: \(\Delta n_1 = n_1 p\), so \(n_1 \to n_1(1+p)\). Nothing in the derivation cares — \(\Delta x\) was arbitrary — but it means \(p\) carries the same meaning whether a variable is of order \(10^{-3}\) or \(10^{5}\), which matters once you start testing across the phase diagram.

\(E_1\) falls by ten per row; \(E_2\) by one hundred. The slope column reads 2. Equation 19 is correct.

F1 = [math.log(abs(r[1])) for r in rows]
F2 = [math.log(abs(r[2])) for r in rows]

def ls_slope(f1, f2):
    m1, m2 = sum(f1) / len(f1), sum(f2) / len(f2)
    return (sum(a * (b_ - m2) for a, b_ in zip(f1, f2))
            / sum(a * (a - m1) for a in f1))

print(f"all five points    slope = {ls_slope(F1, F2):.6f}")
print(f"drop p = 1e-6      slope = {ls_slope(F1[:4], F2[:4]):.6f}")
print(f"p = 1e-2 to 1e-4   slope = {ls_slope(F1[:3], F2[:3]):.6f}")
all five points    slope = 2.000636
drop p = 1e-6      slope = 1.999492
p = 1e-2 to 1e-4   slope = 1.999146

Where it stops working

Look at the last row of the table. The slope column drifts, and the least-squares fit over all five points sits slightly above 2 while the fit over the clean range sits just below.

That is round-off, not a wrong derivative. At \(p = 10^{-6}\) we extract a quantity of order \(10^{-11}\) from a difference of numbers of order \(10^{2}\) — thirteen significant figures, against the sixteen or so double precision carries. Most of the precision is consumed by cancellation before Equation 3 even subtracts.

This is the finite-difference tradeoff, and the consequence is worth stating plainly: shrinking \(\Delta x\) does not sharpen the test indefinitely. Truncation error falls as the step shrinks; round-off error rises. The usable window here runs from about \(10^{-2}\) to \(10^{-4}\), and you find it by looking for where the slope is flat rather than by assuming smaller is better.

Sidestepping the floor

The floor exists because Equation 3 subtracts two nearly equal numbers. If your function is analytic and your code tolerates complex arithmetic, there is a way around it entirely — the complex-step derivative:

\[ \frac{dF}{dx} \approx \frac{\operatorname{Im}\left[F(x + ih)\right]}{h} \tag{20}\]

There is no subtraction of nearly equal reals here, so no cancellation and no floor: take \(h = 10^{-200}\) and the agreement is at machine precision. The catch is that \(F\) must be genuinely analytic. Absolute values, comparisons, branches and anything that discards an imaginary part will break it — and equation-of-state code is full of all four. When it applies it is sharper than anything above; when it fails, the failure itself tells you something about your function.

What a failure looks like

Suppose the last term of Equation 19, \(D f_B b_i\), were wrong by one percent.

A_n1_bad = A_n1 - 0.01 * (D * f_B * b[0])
bad = [(p, E1, E1 - A_n1_bad * n[0] * p) for p, E1, _ in rows]

print("| $p$ range | slope, correct | slope, 1% error |")
print("| --- | --- | --- |")
for k in range(len(rows) - 1):
    good = (math.log(abs(rows[k][2] / rows[k+1][2]))
            / math.log(abs(rows[k][1] / rows[k+1][1])))
    poor = (math.log(abs(bad[k][2] / bad[k+1][2]))
            / math.log(abs(bad[k][1] / bad[k+1][1])))
    print(f"| {rows[k][0]:.0e} to {rows[k+1][0]:.0e} | {good:.4f} | {poor:.4f} |")
\(p\) range slope, correct slope, 1% error
1e-02 to 1e-03 1.9984 1.6216
1e-03 to 1e-04 1.9998 1.1672
1e-04 to 1e-05 2.0001 1.0209
1e-05 to 1e-06 2.0049 1.0021

The correct column holds at 2; the broken one collapses toward 1 as the step shrinks, exactly as the five percent error on \(x^2\) did. One wrong term out of four, under one percent of the total value, and the test reads it off unambiguously.

You would not have caught that by checking the number looked reasonable.

When slope 1 is not a bug

One case breaks that reading. If the derivative you are testing happens to be zero at your test point, \(\frac{dF}{dx}\Delta x\) vanishes, Equation 3 collapses to \(E_2 = E_1\), and the slope is 1 however good your code is:

\(E_1\) and \(E_2\) are not zero here — they are equal, both of order \((\Delta x)^2\), because \(F\) still changes quadratically even where its slope does not. Plotting \(\ln|E_2|\) against \(\ln|E_1|\) is then plotting a quantity against itself, so the slope is 1 for a trivial reason and the derivative was never tested at all.

This is not contrived for an equation of state: \((\partial P/\partial V)_T\) vanishes on the spinodal, and residual properties vanish in the ideal-gas limit. The tell is the ratio. \(E_2/E_1\) pinned at exactly 1 means the derivative returned zero, where a genuine relative error \(p\) gives a constant \(-p\) instead. Report the ratio beside the slope, and move the test point when it reads 1.

“But my derivatives come from a computer”

Worth answering before you build any of this into a test suite.

If you differentiate with autodiff — dual numbers, JAX, reverse mode — the derivative is generated from the function’s own code and is correct to machine precision by construction. The algebra-slip risk genuinely disappears. So is the test pointless?

No, and the clearest evidence is that the autodiff ecosystem ships this exact test. PyTorch’s torch.autograd.gradcheck validates analytical gradients against finite differences, and is the documented way to verify a custom autograd.Function. JAX has check_grads. Both exist because the computer did the differentiation and somebody still wanted proof.

The reason is that the test never asks where the derivative came from. It asks whether your derivative code is consistent with your function code, and that is the same question however the derivative was produced. What changes is what a slope of 1 tells you:

Derivative from What slope 1 means
Hand derivation An algebra slip, or a typo in the code
Symbolic algebra, retyped A transcription error
Autodiff A branch, an internal solver, a custom rule, or the wrong variable
Any of them, months later The function changed and the derivative did not

Where autodiff actually breaks on EOS code

The interesting failures are not in the differentiation. They are in the assumptions around it, and equation-of-state code is unusually rich in them.

Branches. Which cubic root is the liquid? That is an if. Autodiff differentiates the branch taken and tells you nothing about the switch — and near a phase boundary the switch is exactly where you need the derivative to be right.

Internal iterative solves. If the function solves for volume by Newton iteration, naive autodiff differentiates through the iterations instead of applying the implicit function theorem. That is correct only if the iteration converged tightly, and silently wrong otherwise.

Custom derivative rules. The moment you hand-write one for speed you are back to hand-derivation, with less scrutiny, because it looks like autodiff.

Splines and lookup tables. You get the interpolant’s derivative, which is not the property’s derivative.

A symbolic package has a different weakness. It hands you a correct expression, and then a human retypes it as code, splits it across intermediate variables, and reuses a term computed earlier. The derivation was right; the implementation is not.

The reason that never goes away

Someone swaps the \(\alpha\) correlation, or makes \(k_{ij}\) temperature dependent, updates Equation 18 and forgets Equation 19. However the derivative was produced originally, the two code paths have now drifted apart, and this test is what notices.

That makes it a regression test, and regression tests do not care how correct the code was on the day it was written.

What it does not check

It verifies your derivative against your function, not against your intent. Ask for \(\partial/\partial n_i\) when you wanted \(\partial/\partial x_i\), test the same wrong thing, and it passes with slope 2 — consistently differentiating the wrong quantity. The test tells you the two code paths agree. Whether they are the pair you meant is still yours to get right.

Using it in practice

  • Test at several states. A derivative can be right in the vapour region and wrong near the critical point, where \(\alpha(T_r)\) behaves differently.
  • Work outwards. Verify first derivatives before second. A second derivative checked against a wrong first derivative tells you nothing.
  • Perturb relatively, so one \(p\) means the same thing for every variable.
  • Find the flat window before trusting a number, rather than assuming the smallest step is best.
  • Use the free checks too. Mixed second derivatives should be symmetric, \(A_{n_i n_j} = A_{n_j n_i}\), which costs one comparison and no perturbation at all. Euler’s theorem gives another: \(\sum_i n_i A_{n_i}\) should reproduce a quantity you can compute directly.
  • Assert the slope, not the table. Check that the fitted slope from Equation 14 is within tolerance of 2 across the clean window, and the whole thing runs in CI with nobody reading anything.

The idea generalises past equations of state. Any time you differentiate something by hand and then implement it, you have made two independent claims and verified neither. This checks both against the one thing you are sure of.

Reference

The \(E_1\) / \(E_2\) construction and the slope-2 criterion are a standard technique for verifying hand-derived analytical derivatives against finite differences. The SRK Helmholtz formulation in terms of \(B\), \(D\), \(g\) and \(f\) is standard; the worked binary, the multi-function sweep and the round-off analysis are my own.
